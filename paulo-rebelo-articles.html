FedRAMP & AI governance November 24, 2026

FedRAMP taught me something most AI teams are about to learn the hard way: authorization is not a gate at the end. It is a program that runs alongside delivery.

At Trellix, I owned the certification and compliance programs, FedRAMP, DoD, IPv6, that gated over $400M in federal revenue. No authorization, no bid. The lesson was not about paperwork. It was that a control designed in from the start costs a fraction of one retrofitted after the build, when the architecture is already set and the answers are expensive.

The same pattern is showing up in AI now. Teams ship the model, then discover the governance questions: what data trained it, who can see the outputs, what happens when it fails inside a regulated environment. By then, the rework is the project.

In the agentic SDLC work I lead now, we are building it the other way around. Human gates are built into the harness, not written in a prompt. The checkpoint is part of the workflow, not a meeting after it.

The teams that win in regulated AI will not be the ones that move fastest. They will be the ones whose governance moves at the same speed as their delivery.

Decision velocity November 18, 2026

Speed in program delivery isn't about working faster. It's about deciding faster.

I stood up a portfolio command center in hours — not because I rushed, but because every decision had an owner, a date, and a single log to live in.

Decisions didn't wait for the next steering committee. They were made, logged, and moved on.

That log is a repo, not a slide deck. Thirteen folders: command center, RAID, decisions, roadmap, stakeholders, delivery model, policies. Every decision, action, and open question lives in exactly one place — and an AI agent helps me keep it that way.

Last week alone I sent 2,600 requests to Claude from VS Code — 21.8 active hours across 6 days, 90% cache reuse. The agent drafts, I decide.

The programs that stall aren't short on effort. They're short on decision velocity. Where do decisions go to wait in your organization?

AI and technical leadership October 28, 2026

AI is raising the bar for technical leadership. People management alone is no longer enough.
An article featuring Airbnb CEO Brian Chesky makes a timely argument: leaders need to stay directly connected to the work, and professionals need to keep adapting.

My takeaway for TPM Leaders, Heads of PMO, and Engineering Leads: technical fluency should be a core leadership competency.

People leadership remains essential. Empathy, coaching, and trust help teams succeed. Technical depth strengthens that leadership by helping us ask better questions, challenge assumptions, and make informed decisions.

What does that look like in practice?

• TPM Leaders: Understand architecture, APIs, data flows, and dependencies well enough to identify risks that a green status report can hide.
• Heads of PMO: Connect investment and governance decisions to technical feasibility, AI readiness, and measurable business outcomes.
• Engineering Leads: Stay close to design decisions, code quality, reliability, and the evaluation of AI-generated work.

The depth will differ by role. Every leader should understand enough to engage meaningfully with the decisions their teams face.

For AI initiatives, that means being able to ask:
— How will we know the output is trustworthy?
— What data can the system access?
— What happens when it fails?
— Where is human review necessary?
— Does the benefit justify the cost and complexity?

It also means using the tools ourselves: experimenting, building small prototypes, and learning where AI helps and where it struggles.

My view: the strongest leaders will combine people leadership, technical judgment, and business accountability.

As our teams adopt AI, our own learning needs to keep pace.

How are you staying technically engaged as a leader?
